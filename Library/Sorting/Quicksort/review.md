# Review of the specification

Two fresh agents reviewed [the specification](Spec.lean) and [the case inputs](../../../tests/TestSupport/Modules/Quicksort.lean) against [the request](request.txt), from the files alone, each with a Python model of the program at 64-bit words that rebuilt the 83 inputs.  Both found that `expected` states the result the request asks for on every input, the unique nondecreasing permutation under the unsigned order of words, and both found the comment's readings of the order, uniqueness, stability, and the size limit accurate.  Both found that the comment credited `quicksort_bound` alone with the limit on storage and called the storage one block, where `quicksort_implements` bounds the rise of `top` by the generated bound and `quicksort_bound` states that the bound is the cost of one block of 128 words, which the comment now says.  The second review found that the reading of "a call stack that does not grow" rests on a compiler rule that the comment did not name, since Talos bounds no call depth, and the comment now states that the compiler gives a call depth to every recursive function other than a recognized tail recursion.

Both reviews found that arrays of 11 words are the shortest whose partition leaves a part that the steps partition again, where the docstring had called them the shortest that the steps partition, and the first found that "the long arrays" left out those of straight insertion and heapsort, so the docstring now says "the long arrays it adds".  They confirmed the docstring's other claims: the first words of `high` and `low` run the scans of steps Q3 and Q4 to the ends of the subfile at each length, `up 2000` and `down 2000` each go through 1,991 partitions with an empty part, the deepest stack holds 8 entries, and no insertion of step Q9 moves a word more than 8 places.  The second review checked the inputs after the long array of arbitrary words became 10,000 words below 100,000, whose argument of 58,900 bytes fits Linux's limit of 131,072 bytes on one command-line argument, where the first version's 203,990 bytes had stopped the case run.  It noted that the one array larger than a page no longer holds words at or above `2^63`, and that 50 other cases still detect a signed comparison in step Q9.

The reviews ran 28 and 38 wrong programs on the cases.  The cases detect every one whose result differs or whose run does not finish: comparisons in step Q9 that are signed, of 32 bits, or through `Float64`, a step Q9 that is missing, cut short, or never moves a word into position 0, exchanges that copy or lose a word, a scan of step Q3 without its bound, which runs past the end of the array and does not finish on nine inputs, a scan of step Q4 that starts at `e`, and pushes and pops of the wrong entries.  Step Q9 sorts every array, so a defect of steps Q1 to Q8 that keeps the array a permutation and finishes changes no result: a signed comparison in the scans, another `M`, a push of the smaller part, a lost stack entry, an omitted step Q5, or scans that do not stop at equal words.  Such a defect raises the cost alone, for example the moves of step Q9 over the cases from 17,939 to 21.3 million when the stack loses its entries, and the theorems do not exclude it either, since `quicksort_eq` rests on `stages_perm`, which states only that steps Q1 to Q8 permute the array, so the program text alone shows Algorithm Q.

Both reviews recorded classes that no case can reach.  The cases check the result, the live blocks, and the number of allocations, so a stack whose size grew with the array would pass every case, and only `quicksort_bound` excludes it.  The branch for a full stack cannot run, since holding `k` entries needs at least `22·2^(k-1) - 1` words, and only the absence of a call-depth premise in `trapFree` excludes a recursive implementation whose depth stays below 1,000 on every case.
