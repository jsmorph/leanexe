# Review of the specification

Two fresh agents reviewed [the specification](Spec.lean) and [the case inputs](../../../tests/TestSupport/Modules/Find.lean) against [the request](request.txt), from the files alone, each with a Python model of the program at 64-bit words.  The request changed during the reviews: its first draft named Hoare's FIND alone, and it now names the partition steps of quicksort, which the program uses in place of Hoare's partition procedure, and both reviewers re-read it.  Both found that `expected` states the requested result on every input, including repeated words, the empty array, ranks at and past the length, and ranks of `2^32` or more, and the second checked it against the model on all cases and on 85,142 random inputs.  Both found that the registry already gave `Verified.Examples.Select` the module name `select`, so that the two modules overwrote one file, and the module is now `find`.

Both found that the comment's "a module that allocates nothing, which `select_bound` states" attributed the statement about `top` to the equation of the bound and said more than the theorems.  `select_implements` with `select_bound` states that a call does not raise `top`, and the heap's free list could serve an allocation without raising it, so the comment now reads "in place" as a call that does not raise `top`.  The second review found the sentence on call nesting hard to read, and it now says that a theorem without a depth premise covers code with no recursion other than loops, whose calls nest no deeper than the longest chain of calls, which the quicksort comment now says as well.  The first review found that for a rank past the array the program still partitioned the whole array, which the request's method does not describe, so `select` now returns 0 for such a rank without partitioning.  It also found that the rank `2^32 + 1` detects a program that reads the rank in 32 bits, where the docstring had named a comparison of the low bits, which changes no result, and the docstring is corrected.

Both confirmed the docstring's other claims: the arrays of at most 12 words take every rank from 0 to the length, the longer arrays take four ranks, the first partitions of the arrays of 10 and 11 words in order place the smallest or the largest word, and the word of rank 1 of the ten words is not 0.  The second review ran 44 wrong programs and the first a similar set, and the cases detect every one that changes a result: signed or 32-bit comparisons of words in either scan, the wrong part after a partition, a single partition, stops at subfiles of two or more words, reads of the wrong position or of the input array, other results for a rank past the array, an omitted step Q5, wrong exchanges, and a scan of step Q3 without its bound, which runs past the end of the array and does not finish.  The second review found that no case needed more than 1,999 partitions, so a limit of that size on their number went undetected, and the middle rank of 10,000 words in decreasing order, which needs about 10,000 partitions, now detects it.

Both reviews recorded defects that no input can detect, since they change no result: scans that do not stop at equal words, a loop that continues after a partition places its word at `k`, a partitioning word taken from position `k`, a cutoff followed by a sort of the last subfile, and a signed or 32-bit comparison of the rank inside `find` alone.  Such differences from the request's method show only in the program text, and the theorems do not constrain them either, since `select_eq` states the result alone.  Positions that wrap at `2^16` would need arrays of 65,536 words, whose decimal form exceeds the host's limit of 128 KiB on one command-line argument.
