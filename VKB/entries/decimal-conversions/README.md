# Decimal text of floating-point values

A program that reads or writes numbers as decimal text uses the library's parser, `Library/Numerics/Decimal/ParseFloat`, and printer, `Library/Numerics/Decimal/PrintFloat`, or proves its own handling of text against them.  Their theorems state which strings the parser accepts and the binary64 or binary32 word it returns for each, what the printer writes, that the parser reads the printed text back, and that the printed decimal is the shortest that rounds to the word.  The entry applies to a proof about these programs, to a program that calls them, and to a new conversion of the same kind.

The specification has three parts, all in `Library/Numerics/Decimal/Shared`.  `Library.Numerics.Decimal.Shared.StrconvGrammar.Derives general s n` is the grammar as an inductive relation with one constructor for each production: RFC 8259's `number` when `general` is false, and a superset in the style of C's `strtod`, with hexadecimal significands, infinities, and NaN, when it is true.  `Library.Numerics.Decimal.Shared.StrconvDecode.decode` reads the same strings as a function on lists with unbounded integers, `derives_iff_decode` states that the relation is the decoder's graph, and `derives_unique` follows.  `Library.Numerics.Decimal.Shared.StrconvRound.round64 n` is the word of roundTiesToEven applied to the number's value, with the sign applied after the rounding, `canon64` replaces every NaN by the canonical NaN, and `Shortest64 w neg d e` states that a decimal rounds to `w` and comes first among such decimals by its count of significant digits, its distance from `w`, and an even last digit.

A string is an array of words that hold one byte each, `StrconvGrammar.words s`.  The programs read every byte through `Library.Numerics.Arithmetic.Uscale.limb`, which returns 0 past the end, `Library.Numerics.Decimal.ParseFloat.ParseFloatScan.limb_words` reads byte `j` of `words s` as `byteAt s j`, and `Library.Numerics.Arithmetic.Uscale.UscaleTheorem.limb_eq` equates `limb` with a read that returns the default element past the end.  `decide +kernel` checks a fact about a class of bytes at each of the 256 bytes: `ParseFloatScan.byte_cases` reduces a statement about a variable byte to one over `n < 256`, as `ParseFloatScan.isDigitW_byte` shows for the digit test.

The parser's theorem goes through the decoder.  The proof relates the scanner's positions to the decoder's splits of the list (`ParseFloatScan.span_spec`, `Library.Numerics.Decimal.ParseFloat.ParseFloatTheorem.sign_scan`), and the value is the correctly rounded word that the `correct-rounding` entry describes.  `ParseFloatTheorem.parse64_spec` states `parse64 general (words s) = res64 (decode general s)` for every string of fewer than `2 ^ 32` bytes, and `parse64_accept` and `parse64_reject` restate it through the grammar with `derives_iff_decode`, as `parse32_accept` and `parse32_reject` do for binary32.  The section "Proof" of `Library/Numerics/Decimal/ParseFloat/README.md` lists the parts of the proof module by module.

The printer's theorems compare layouts.  A layout is a concatenation of runs of digits and fixed bytes, and `Library.Numerics.Decimal.PrintFloat.PrintFloatLayout.build_words` equates an array that `LeanExe.build` fills byte by byte with `words l`, so that, with `words_append`, the proof compares the runs one at a time.  `Library.Numerics.Decimal.PrintFloat.PrintFloatTheorem.print64_derives` states that the printed bytes form a number of the general grammar that rounds to `canon64 w`, `print64_roundtrip` follows from it and `parse64_accept`, and `print64_shortest` states that a finite nonzero word prints as a `Shortest64` decimal of RFC 8259's grammar.

A program that handles text around the library reduces its own handling to the library's statements and then applies them unchanged.  `Demos.RunningSumFp.stripCR_words` proves, through `build_words`, that the running sum's removal of a final carriage return yields `words (trimCR input)`.  `Demos.RunningSumFp.parse_accept` is then `parse64_accept` at the trimmed line, and `render_roundtrip` and `render_shortest` apply `print64_roundtrip` and `print64_shortest` to the printed line.

`split_ifs` also splits the `if` of the sign, so `ParseFloatTheorem.dec64` generalizes the sign word `if neg = true then 0x8000000000000000 else 0` to a variable before the case split.  `decide` refuses a goal with free variables, and `byte_cases` removes the variable byte before `decide +kernel` runs.  Native Lean prints a panic message with a backtrace at each read past an array's end, and a first native check of the limb arithmetic, whose comparison and subtraction read the shorter operand past its end, ran slowly under that output, so `limb` tests the position before it reads.

Stating `Library.Numerics.Decimal.ParseFloat.ParseFloatHexParse.hexParse_spec` exposed an exponent saturated at `10 ^ 10`, which is too small for hexadecimal input.  A fraction of `n` hexadecimal digits lowers the binary exponent by `4 n`, and the hypothesis of fewer than `2 ^ 32` bytes allows fractions long enough that a saturated exponent would round a finite value to zero.  `Library.Numerics.Decimal.ParseFloat.expDigits` and `ParseFloatScan.satE` saturate at `10 ^ 11`.

The parser's chain is `parse64_spec`, `parse64_accept`, and `parse64_reject`, and its byte theorems in `ParseFloatBytes.lean` transfer the results to the compiled module as the `real-function-accuracy` entry describes for the exponential, with a trap allowed when an allocation finds no memory.  The printer's chain is `print64_derives`, `print64_roundtrip`, and `print64_shortest`.  The running sum of `Demos/RunningSumFp/ArithmeticProof.lean` is a consumer of both.
